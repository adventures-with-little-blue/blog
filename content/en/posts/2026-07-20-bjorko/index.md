# The World Heritage Site Kvarken Archipelago

I took an evening ride out to the nearest of UNESCO's World Heritage sites, namely the [Kvarken Archipelago](https://www.maailmanperinto.fi/en/kvarken-archipelago/). Together with the High Coast in Sweden, parts of the Kvarken Archipelago form a shared World Heritage site. So far it is the only natural heritage site in Finland. Perhaps the best place to admire the peculiarities and beauty of this natural heritage is Svedjehamn with its fine lookout tower [Saltkaret](https://korsholm.fi/kommun/turism/sevardheter/saltkaret-utsiktstorn) (sv).

<!--more-->

This was of course not the first time I had visited Svedjehamn, but until now I had never managed to arrive in time for a café visit at Salteriet. I did not manage it this time either. It was almost 9 PM before I parked in the large parking area, which requires a parking fee out here in the countryside. So all I could do was walk off toward the lookout tower to enjoy the view.

![Parking](saltkaret1.jpg "Parking with a view toward Saltkaret.")

It is a walk of just under ten minutes from the parking area to the lookout tower. The road is in good condition, but for understandable reasons vehicle traffic is limited so that only landowners may drive out to the boat sheds. There was a bit of a breeze, which kept most of the mosquitoes away. But it was still warm enough in the thick riding trousers.

![Saltkaret](saltkaret2.jpg "The Saltkaret lookout tower.")

![Saltkaret](saltkaret3.jpg "Saltkaret.")

What makes Kvarken's natural heritage special is that you can see land uplift taking place here almost in real time. When the inland ice sheet was at its heaviest, it pressed the earth's crust downward, and even today the land continues to rise as that weight has disappeared. In Kvarken the uplift is roughly eight millimeters per year, which means that new skerries, islets and coastal meadows slowly rise from the sea. The landscape is therefore unusually young and also constantly changing.

The other really distinctive feature is the so-called De Geer moraines. These are long, narrow moraine ridges that were formed at the ice margin toward the end of the last Ice Age. Around Svedjehamn they lie in the landscape like a washboard and continue out into the water in almost parallel rows. This unusually clear series of moraine ridges is one of the things that makes Kvarken geologically so special. The De Geer moraines are most clearly visible from the lookout tower.

After enjoying the view, I wandered around for a while among the boat sheds in Svedjehamn. Here people are in a constant struggle against the land uplift. Harbors must be moved or dredged every few decades. A boat shed that stood right by the shoreline a hundred years ago can today stand well up on dry land.

On the way home I made a short detour via Björköby church. One interesting thing in Björköby is their road names. Some of them have the suffix _-ted_, which seems to mean something like _road_. This term is probably not used anywhere else.

![Björköby church](bjorkoby_kyrka.jpg "Björköby church.")

![Björköby road names](bjorkoby_vagnamn.jpg "Läsarted and Uppbyggted.")

Björkö was for a long time a community without a road connection to the mainland. All transport took place by boat. The road to Replot was not opened until 1954, when the embankment across Skalörfjärden was completed. Replot, in turn, had received regular ferry traffic to the mainland in 1952. The fixed road connection over [Replot Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replot_Bridge) was opened in 1997 and then replaced the road ferry for good.
