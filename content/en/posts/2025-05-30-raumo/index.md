# Nuclear Coffee and a Breath of the Middle Ages

Visiting Rauma has been on my wish list for a long time. Today it was finally time. The weather was fine when I set off at 8 in the morning, but unfortunately rain was forecast for the evening. I also brought along a new toy, an Aoocci C3 touchscreen with Android Auto support. So now I had both voice navigation through the headset and visual navigation that did not depend on taking my eyes off the road and glancing down at the phone in the tank bag. I had also brought my interchangeable-lens camera to see how well that works when travelling on two wheels.

<!--more-->

### Transit Stretch

I started by following Highway 8 south. Quite dull, but with a long day ahead of me I needed to save time. I made a small detour into Lappfjärd and then headed back to the main road. From Härkmeri onward, though, the plan was to avoid the highway. I rode through Härkmeri and then took a short break in Kilen. A sailboat with its crew was lying in the harbour, but apart from that the place was completely deserted. The summer season probably had not really started yet.

### The Satakunta Coast

I then continued south and soon crossed the border into Satakunta. That border is also a fairly sharp language boundary. Sideby is bilingual, probably with a Swedish-speaking majority, whereas Merikarvia is entirely Finnish-speaking. It was not always like that, and a large number of Swedish or Swedish-derived place names bear witness to the fact that just over a hundred years ago there was still Swedish-speaking settlement all the way down toward Rauma. I passed through places such as Kasaböle (fi: Kasala), Risby (fi: Riispyy), Sastmola (fi: Merikarvia), Ytterby (fi: Alakylä), and Norrsågen (fi: Pohjansaha), before riding a little way out toward Sandö via Vittisbofjärd (fi: Ahlainen). Ahlainen seemed like a pleasant place, certainly worth a return visit at some point. The road itself was also very enjoyable. All the way from Sideby down to Ämtö was fairly narrow, winding asphalt. In places it had clearly been patched, but overall it was in reasonably good condition. There was not much traffic either.

At Ämtö I joined Road 272 toward Reposaari (sv: Räfsö) and Mäntyluoto. I took a ride through Reposaari and had a look around. I had considered stopping there for lunch, but with the clock still a little before 11 it felt too early. So I carried on. I skipped Kallo lighthouse and the small district of Uniluoto, thinking I could come back another time. To save time, I also rode straight past Yyteri (sv: Ytterö) beach. I had been there before, and I still had many kilometres of riding ahead of me that day.

![Reposaari](reposaari.jpg "View from Reposaari toward Mäntyluoto.")

In any case, the next step was a few kilometres along Highway 2 toward Pori. I turned off toward Preiviikki (sv: Bredvik) to take the "back way" to Eurajoki and Rauma, without returning to the highway again. After that came a whole string of intersections and different roads that I could never have memorised without a navigator. And if the navigator had given up, I would hardly even have known where I was. In any case, I rode through places like Makolma (sv: Maggholm) and Sunti (sv: Södersund) before arriving in Luvia. At Luvia church I turned south again, via Luodonkylä (sv: Norrby), Lemlahti (sv: Lemlax), and Kuivalahti (sv: Kvivlax). There was a short stretch of gravel here, but otherwise the road was paved, though much of it was patched and full of bumps.

Next I arrived at Vuojoki Manor. The manor appears to belong to the town of Eurajoki, but it is apparently rented out to Areva and perhaps others. Nuclear builders and operators, in other words. The building itself was locked, but the grounds were open to visitors. It was a very elegant manor, and it seemed to be in fairly good condition. I took the chance to sit down in the sunshine, eat the lunch I had brought with me, and drink coffee from the thermos.

### Olkiluoto

From Vuojoki Manor I continued to one of the main goals of the day: the Olkiluoto nuclear power plant, more specifically the visitor centre. It was an impressive place, even if the exhibition itself was fairly dull. There was certainly plenty to read and poke at, but nothing especially surprising or exciting. It would probably not be worth bringing children here; I think the place is better suited to upper-secondary or university students. One thing I did appreciate, though, was the free machine coffee, no doubt brewed with locally produced electricity. And the nuclear coffee could be enjoyed out on the terrace with a view of the three nuclear reactors.

![Olkiluoto reactors](olkiluoto4.jpg "Olkiluoto's three reactors. From left to right: OL3, OL1, and OL2.")

![Model of Olkiluoto 3](olkiluoto2.jpg "Model of Olkiluoto 3.")

![Storage canisters](olkiluoto3.jpg "Storage canisters for spent nuclear fuel. Graphite with a copper shell, then 400 metres down into the bedrock.")

![Nuclear coffee](karnkaffe.jpg "'Nuclear coffee'")

Beside the road leading to the visitor centre and the plant's main gate there is another much-talked-about site as well: the entrance to Onkalo, the storage facility for nuclear waste blasted into the bedrock. The actual "entrance" was in fact a rather large building that, besides lifts, ventilation and the like, probably also houses the machinery used to transfer the nuclear waste containers.


### Rauma

From the Olkiluoto road it was only a short distance before the built-up area of Rauma began. I deliberately left the route to see more of the town and ended up at Kiikartorni, which I had not managed to scout out in advance. Definitely worth a short stop.

![Kiikartorni](raumo1.jpg "Kiikartorni.")

I then headed into Old Rauma, parked near the market square, and wandered around for a while. By now the weather was on the warm side for my winter-lined motorcycle trousers, so I rather quickly sought out a local pizzeria for lunch.

_UPDATE 2026:_ Looking back, it feels worth adding that [Old Rauma](https://www.maailmanperinto.fi/en/old-rauma/) is one of Finland's UNESCO World Heritage Sites. It is the largest continuous preserved wooden town area in the Nordic countries. The oldest houses date from the early 18th century. No grey concrete high-rises from the 1970s, or anything similar, spoil the coherent character of the place.

![Kitukränn](raumo2.jpg "Kitukränn, the narrowest street in Finland. I assume the name comes from the Swedish 'Skitu-gränd' ('Shit-alley'), despite Wikipedia claiming otherwise.")

![Pizza in Old Rauma](pizza.jpg "Pizza in Old Rauma.")

When lunch was over, I returned to the motorcycle. I had locked the helmet onto a hook under the seat, and I had apparently left the ignition key in the seat lock. Well then, mostly honest people in Rauma. I swung back into the saddle and slowly found my way out of the old town, now heading toward the centre of Eurajoki.


### Inland Satakunta

Along small and pleasant roads I eventually came to the village of Panelia. Besides its amusing name, the village has a whole cluster of old, beautiful buildings. From there I headed toward Harjavalta, once again avoiding major Highway 43 and sticking to smaller roads instead.

![Panelia](panelia.jpg "Pee break in Panelia.")

In Harjavalta I skipped the idea of going to see Boliden's monstrous copper and nickel plant. Instead I went to admire the Harjavalta hydroelectric power station, which dams the Kokemäki River with an impressive 26-metre drop. Strictly speaking, the power station is probably just across the municipal boundary in Nakkila. A single-lane road for car traffic runs right across the top of the station. It is rather odd that this works at all, with no passing places or traffic lights.

![Harjavalta power station](harjavalta1.jpg "Harjavalta power station.")

![Harjavalta power station](harjavalta2.jpg "Harjavalta power station.")

In Nakkila it started to rain. I thought it would only be a brief shower; after all, the rain was not supposed to begin until late in the evening. But, as so often this summer, the rain was in a hurry to start and in no hurry at all to stop. In Ulvila I refuelled. The rain kept coming, so at Ulvila old church, St. Olaf's Church as it is called, I stopped and pulled on the rain suit, boot covers and all. The church was unfortunately locked, otherwise it would have been an interesting place to visit.

![Ulvila old church](ulvsby.jpg "Ulvila St. Olaf's Church.")

The next destination was Noormarkku and the Noormarkku ironworks area. Despite having driven past the signs for Noormarkku by car countless times, I had never actually visited the place before, which was an oversight. Besides the ironworks buildings themselves, there are several grand old residential buildings that the Ahlström family built for themselves along the Noormarkku River. I strolled around for a while in the drizzle. I would gladly come back here another day when the sun is shining.

![Noormarkku](norrmark3.jpg "Head office of the Ahlström companies.")

![Noormarkku](norrmark2.jpg "Head office of the Ahlström companies.")

![Noormarkku](norrmark4.jpg "Noormarkku ironworks and hydroelectric power station.")

Despite the rain, I decided to stick to the planned route. I rode along Highway 23 toward Kankaanpää. I made a detour through Pomarkku to look at the two churches there, the old one and the new one, which stand right next to each other. I then rode through Kankaanpää and followed winding little roads to Jämi airfield. The airfield itself was not especially interesting, but it was fun to see the much-talked-about ski tunnel.


### Homeward

From there I continued through the centre of Jämijärvi to Niinisalo, where I turned north toward Karvia. In Karvia I decided to take a slightly different route from the one I had planned and ride via Kauhajärvi before continuing on to Kauhajoki. But since it was raining and my gloves were soaked through (Gore-Tex!) and difficult to take off and put back on, I could not be bothered entering the new route into the navigator. I'll find my way, I thought. And naturally I got it wrong. Instead, I ended up riding toward Nummijärvi. Perhaps just as well, because both routes included some gravel road, which is a poor choice in persistent rain. The road was quite slippery, and it felt very long, though in reality it was only about 10 km, before the asphalt began again. By the time I reached Kauhajoki I was dirty, wet, and tired, and the heavy cloud cover was making it fairly dark as well. So I stopped for coffee at ABC before the last stretch. The coffee perked me up, and I made it home safely via Jurva.

This ride ended up being 685 km long. I started early and got home late. If you want to ride much farther than this in a single day, you probably need to stick to bigger roads or skip some of the stops, but where would the fun be in that?


### What I Learned This Time

The new screen, the Aoocci C3, worked quite well. Unfortunately, I did not manage to angle it directly toward myself. In bright light the screen simply is not bright enough, so I have to lean forward over it to see properly. I am considering building a mount for it so it can sit above the instruments, aimed straight at me. The touchscreen is difficult to use with gloves on, and with my winter gloves, which have no touchscreen support, it is impossible. Android Auto itself, however, worked flawlessly. I followed the whole journey with my Kurviger app without any problems.

I also tried bringing my old interchangeable-lens camera along on the trip. That worked only moderately well in the tank bag, as it was rather cumbersome to pull out. It was not possible to use it with gloves on either, so in that respect it was no better than the phone camera. Once the rain started, I certainly did not have the energy to dig it out from under the tank bag's rain cover. And in the end, the pictures are probably not all that much better with the camera than with the phone anyway. It is still the same photographer.

This time the rain gear worked fairly well. My torso, arms, legs, and feet stayed reasonably dry, though everything felt a little damp. No large amount of water got in anywhere, not even around the neck. I simply should have put the rain suit on as soon as it started raining, and I might have avoided some of that damp feeling. My supposedly rainproof Richa Gore-Tex gloves, on the other hand, were basically soaked through. The Pinlock visor stayed almost completely clear. A little fogging appeared around the edges toward the end, but not enough to interfere seriously with visibility.

I more or less stuck to my planned route, despite the rain starting much earlier than the forecast had promised. It worked out fine, but riding in the rain is undeniably less enjoyable. You have to be more careful and cannot enjoy curvy roads in quite the same way, and you also see less of the surroundings. Above all, the rain becomes an obstacle because taking gloves and other gear off and on is such a nuisance that you end up skipping short stops when something interesting appears. And if you do stop, you often cannot be bothered digging out the camera.

Once you have slipped into "just follow the navigator" mode, it is completely hopeless to imagine that you will suddenly be able to navigate from memory instead. Studying the map beforehand certainly gives you a general sense of how the roads run, but remembering every junction and every turn seems to be too much to ask, at least of me. Hence the navigational mistake. And perhaps it really is worth trying to avoid gravel roads in the rain. They are slow going, they get everything dirty, and in places they can be quite slippery as well.
