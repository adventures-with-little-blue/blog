# Servicing the front forks

When I bought the bike a few years ago, the seller told me that the front forks had recently been rebuilt. Even so, the front forks have now been leaking oil for quite some time. I have tried to fix it by slipping a thin plastic film between the forks and the oil seal, but the last time it did not help at all. Lately there have even been oil stains on the garage floor under the front forks. It also felt as though the suspension no longer worked as it should, with both vibrations and a kind of "bouncing" on uneven road surfaces. So it was time to deal with this necessary chore: servicing the front forks.

<!--more-->

It was the first time I had taken off and dismantled the front forks, so there was a bit of pondering before I got started. I removed the right fork leg first and took it apart. No real problems, but a bit of mess with the oil and so on - and the oil in those forks was awful - both in appearance and smell! I also made the mistake of leaving oily newspaper lying around in my combined office/workshop, which meant the place stank of that horrible oil the next day when I was sitting at my computer trying to work.

![One-legged Blue](enbent_blue.jpg "Little Blue is one-legged. The right fork leg removed. The brake caliper is secured properly so that all the weight does not hang from the brake hose.")

![Oil](gaffelolja.jpg "Old fork oil - ugh, what a mess!")

When I inspected the parts I could see that most of them were in reasonably good condition. The guide bushes were admittedly a little worn, but they should probably last a good while yet. The springs looked fine. The O-ring at the fork bolt was also in good condition, which was fortunate because I had not bought a new one. The dust seal really ought to be replaced every time, but I did not have one either, so the old one would have to do. The plan, then, was only to replace the oil seal and fork oil, while reusing all the other wear parts.

![Parts](gaffelben1.jpg "The fork leg taken apart.")

Once I had got the fork leg apart, things stalled a little. There were a few things I needed to sort out before I could continue:
  * The chrome-plated inner tube had a few irregularities, so I had to find out how those could be dealt with. Really the whole tube ought to be re-chromed, but that is a slow and surely expensive story. After a bit of research I decided just to use fine sandpaper (P800 and P1200) and sand around the tube - not lengthwise. But first I had to buy the sandpaper.
  * To clean the parts from oil and dirt I had planned to use Brakleen, but the internet advised against that and recommended isopropanol instead. It has been on the "should buy" list for a long time, so now it was time to do something about it and at the same time get a spray bottle to distribute the volatile liquid.
  * Then I had to figure out how to drive the oil seal into the fork leg. Buying an expensive special tool (and waiting weeks for delivery) did not feel like an option. Although I remembered that I ought to have some suitable thick PVC pipe lying around somewhere, I could not find any. Instead I found a tip saying that you can wrap many turns of electrical tape around the fork and use the fork itself as a sliding weight. I decided to try that. And I was able to find electrical tape in my own supplies.
  * To measure the oil level in the tube, I decided to simply dip an ordinary retractable tape measure into it and read off where the oil level was.

Once the sandpaper and IPA had been bought, I got on with cleaning the parts and then putting everything back together. It went fairly well. The biggest problem was tightening the bottom bolt to the correct torque - I had to screw the fork leg onto a piece of wood to keep it stable enough. I used an oiled plastic bag over the inner tube to protect the oil seal as I slid it onto the tube. The electrical-tape driver turned out to work really well - there was quite a lot of resistance when knocking the inner tube into the outer tube, but in the end the seal sank into place. I rolled the tape back onto the roll so I could reuse it on the next fork leg. With the help of a measuring jug I managed to fill exactly the right amount of oil - almost to the millimetre.

![Improvised vise](skruvstads_surrogat.jpg "A vise would have been useful, but lacking one I had to screw the fork leg down in order to tighten the bottom bolt to the correct torque.")

![Seal driver](tatningsdrivare1.jpg "Rolling my own fork seal driver.")

![Seal in place](tatningsdrivare2.jpg "Now the oil seal is in place. The groove for the retaining ring is visible as it should be.")

After that it was just a matter of fitting the fork leg back in place and then taking on the left fork leg. The second time went a little faster, although I ran into a few small problems. This time the damper assembly rotated when I tried to tighten the bottom bolt. To keep it from moving, I put the spring and the spacer tube back in and screwed on the fork bolt - then it was possible to tighten it to the correct torque. This time the measuring jug gave me a little too little oil, so I had to add and remove a bit of oil before the air gap in the tube was correct.

After that I mounted the other fork leg and adjusted the headlight bracket to the correct height on both sides. I gave the front brake caliper and disc a somewhat cursory cleaning and then mounted the caliper onto the fork leg. The service manual says the bolts should be replaced, but I just put a little Loctite on the old ones. After that I put the front wheel back in place, then the front mudguard, and finally the speedometer cable. Done!

By the time I was finished it was already past midnight, so there was not really time for a proper test ride. I took the test ride the next day instead, at the same time as I headed out for lunch. After that short test ride I can perhaps conclude that it is better than before. The "bouncing" was gone and the front end somehow felt calmer. So far there are no signs of oil leakage. But the vibrations are not gone. Maybe it is a little better, but there is probably something else causing the vibrations. My next step will be to try synchronising the carburettors - even though I do not think the issue is engine vibration. As I have noted before, with a bike this old there will probably be more tinkering than riding.
